\title{TrustSECO: Building Software Trust through Community Consensus}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Authors}}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Software reuse depends on trust in components, their maintainers, and the communities that sustain them. Yet the decision to adopt a dependency leaves individual developers and organizations to decide whose assessments they can rely on. This paper introduces TrustSECO, a platform for building software trust through community consensus. Contributors jointly maintain the information used to assess components, while a trust model connects project health, vulnerability severity, and recovery over time. Participation, rewards, and accountable review support the community that produces these assessments. Building on the npmSECO evaluation of trust information within package selection, we position TrustSECO as shared infrastructure for dependency decisions: an organization could use its own priorities to steer developers towards alternatives or security review. The tool demonstration shows how a component's assessment is formed, explained, and challenged. We present the platform and its trust model, and outline a research agenda for making community-based trust part of everyday software engineering.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Choosing a dependency means placing trust in people and software beyond the boundaries of one's own project. Hou and Jansen define software trust as ``the willingness of SECO actors to accept risks based on subjective beliefs''~\cite[Sec.~4.1.6]{hou2023systematic}. These beliefs concern whether a component will serve its purpose, whether its maintainers will respond to problems, and whether the surrounding ecosystem will continue to support it. Trust therefore matters both when software is selected and when continued reliance on it must be reconsidered.

Research has begun to make these relationships explicit. Boughton et al.~\cite{boughton2024decomposing} connect trust relationships to observable behaviour, while Beller and Hejderup~\cite{beller2019blockchain} envision shared software engineering infrastructure supported by distributed ledgers. These perspectives motivate a further question: how can developers and organizations collectively sustain the trust on which software reuse depends? A useful answer must connect assessment to the moment of decision and explain how the community behind that assessment remains accountable.

This paper introduces TrustSECO, a platform for building software trust through community consensus. Its central proposition is that the work of assessing shared dependencies can itself become a shared undertaking. Contributors collect information about components, reach agreement on the observations used in assessments, and receive rewards for accepted contributions. Developers can inspect the resulting trust assessment and challenge its basis. Organizations can, in the longer term, interpret these shared assessments according to their own priorities and risk tolerance.

TrustSECO advances the earlier infrastructure vision~\cite{hou2021trustseco} through a working platform that brings together three elements: a community process for maintaining trust information, a scoring model that responds differently to trust gained and trust lost, and a developer-facing explanation of each assessment. Community consensus establishes an agreed basis for assessment; it does not require all organizations to make the same adoption decision. The contribution is this connection between collective assessment and situated software selection. The demonstration makes the approach tangible, while its effects on developer confidence and dependency choices remain questions for empirical study.

\section{Trust in Dependency Decisions}
\label{sec:workflow}
Trust assessment should be part of adding a dependency. npmSECO already evaluated this approach: it displayed trust information before npm installation and received encouraging usability feedback through interviews with 20 software engineers~\cite{hou2024npmseco}.

Building on that experience, our intended workflow checks a selected version against an organizational threshold. A low score prompts exploration of alternatives or deeper security-team assessment, with blocking determined by policy. This policy integration extends the evaluated approach. The developer can then examine why the component falls below the threshold and what would justify a different decision. The demonstration uses Flask to show the assessment and community history supporting this interaction.

\subsection{Understanding and acting on trust}
The component view (Figure~\ref{fig:flask-detail}) explains a trust assessment through its constituent measures, their freshness, and the extent of community agreement. It distinguishes provisional reports from confirmed inputs and makes missing information visible. This allows a developer or security reviewer to ask a practical question: does the concern warrant choosing another component, gathering more information, or accepting the dependency in this particular context?

The platform supports this reasoning without treating a score as a guarantee of suitability. Repository measures describe a project's observed condition, while vulnerability advisories concern affected package versions. An organization may therefore reach different decisions for different uses of the same component. Package-manager enforcement and security-review routing are intended integrations; the demonstrated tool provides the assessment and explanation on which they would depend.

Developers can help shape the information that they and others rely on. An admitted contributor collects observations, attaches its identity to them, and participates in the community's assessment process. The platform records who contributed, how agreement was reached, and whether a contribution was subsequently challenged. This participation turns dependency assessment into an ongoing relationship between software users and the community maintaining the trust information.

\subsection{A model of trust gained and lost}
TrustSECO treats the accumulation and loss of trust differently: positive evidence contributes a bounded baseline, while vulnerabilities affecting the selected version can sharply reduce the score. For a nonnegative measurement $x_i$ and reference $a_i>0$, the baseline is
\begin{equation}
 z_i=\begin{cases}x_i/(x_i+a_i),&\text{higher is better},\\a_i/(x_i+a_i),&\text{lower is better},\end{cases}
 \quad B=100\sum_{i\in I}w_i z_i.
\label{eq:normalization}
\end{equation}
Here $I$ is the set of available indicators, $z_i\in[0,1]$ their normalized values, and $w_i\geq0$ their weights, summing to one over all configured indicators. The model combines maintenance activity, release recency, contributor participation, project maturity, and ecosystem use. Normalization bounds each contribution so that a large value for one measure cannot dominate the assessment. Missing measures reduce coverage instead of being mistaken for favourable observations.

For distinct applicable advisories $V$, with severity multipliers $m_v$ and ceilings $c_v$, the target and upward recovery rule are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 Q_t&=\min\left(B_t\prod_{v\in V}m_v,\;\min(\{100\}\cup\{c_v:v\in V\})\right),\\
 S_t&=\min(Q_t,\;S_{t-1}+\rho\Delta_t),\qquad \rho=\TrustRecovery.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
The set $V$ contains distinct advisories affecting the selected version. Each multiplier $m_v\in[0,1]$ reduces the baseline, and each ceiling $c_v$ limits the score despite positive project characteristics. The resulting target is $Q_t$. The displayed score $S_t$ can fall immediately but recovers at rate $\rho$ only over intervals $\Delta_t$ supported by fresh observations. This expresses the intuition that a serious vulnerability can rapidly undermine confidence, whereas regaining it requires sustained grounds for reliance. The current recovery rate is \TrustRecovery{} point per supported day; the parameters are design choices to be evaluated, not calibrated probabilities of software safety.

The \href{https://www.first.org/cvss/v4.0/specification-document}{Common Vulnerability Scoring System (CVSS)} characterizes vulnerabilities and rates their severity. TrustSECO uses these severity classes for its penalties: with a baseline of 80, one high-severity advisory yields 40 and one critical advisory yields 20. The U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) maintains a \href{https://www.cisa.gov/known-exploited-vulnerabilities-catalog}{catalog of vulnerabilities exploited in real attacks}; an applicable entry imposes a stricter ceiling. \href{https://osv.dev/}{Open Source Vulnerabilities (OSV)} aggregates advisories across open-source ecosystems and connects them to affected packages and versions.

An assessment also expresses what the community does not yet know. Weighted coverage is $C=\sum_{i\in I}w_i$, giving a baseline interval $[B,B+100(1-C)]$ when positive indicators are missing. The score uses the lower bound and is withheld when coverage, freshness, security information, or supported history is insufficient. A new release does not erase a project's assessment history, and gaps in observations do not earn recovery. Corrections remain possible through accountable review. The distinction between an unfavourable assessment and an unavailable one is essential: uncertainty should prompt further inquiry.

In the longer term, organizations should be able to adjust the weights to emphasize the characteristics they value most, such as maintenance, security, or ecosystem support. An organization $o$ would apply a versioned weight profile $w_i^{(o)}$ in Equation~\ref{eq:normalization} to the same shared evidence. The profile should accompany the score so that its interpretation remains reproducible; scores from different profiles need not be directly comparable. Organization-specific configuration is a planned capability, not a feature of the current platform.

\section{Building Trust through Community Consensus}
\label{sec:community}
Community consensus provides a shared basis for assessing components. It addresses agreement about the information used to judge software, while leaving the final decision to developers and organizations. TrustSECO combines admission, repeated observation, dispute handling, and incentives so that this shared basis can be maintained and revised over time.

Participation requires a persistent identity and admission review. Contributors demonstrate control of a signing key and link it to an established GitHub account; a designated governor reviews whether they represent distinct operators. Duplicate identities do not create additional votes. These controls address Sybil attacks, in which one actor attempts to gain influence through many identities. They depend on the quality of admission review: account ownership alone cannot establish independence.

\subsection{Consensus on shared observations}
For each requested measurement, contributors submit observations within a collection round. At least three admitted contributors must agree within a defined tolerance before the measurement can be confirmed. The platform requires an unambiguous group whose members are pairwise compatible and uses its upper median as the representative value. Competing groups leave the measurement disputed. This makes disagreement part of the assessment process rather than concealing it behind an average.

Community consensus and ledger consensus serve complementary purposes. The former determines which observations support an assessment; the latter maintains a consistent history of contributions and decisions. Confirmation requires both agreement and a finalized record. Neither guarantees the correctness of an external source. The model assumes sufficiently independent contributors and remains exposed to collusion; stronger assignment and admission mechanisms are avenues for further development.

\subsection{Review, suspension, and appeal}
An outlying observation alone does not establish misconduct. The governor must substantiate an incident against an observation in a closed round, recording evidence, a reason, and a root cause. Under the initial policy, three distinct substantiated incidents within 30 days flag a contributor for review; five suspend participation in verification and reward eligibility. Reports associated with the same root cause or round do not accumulate additional strikes.

Suspension excludes the contributor's observations from supporting current verification results. Appeals, overturned incidents, and explicit reinstatement remain in the audit history. Compromised keys can be revoked. These mechanisms allow the community to respond to unreliable contributions while preserving the record of both the original evidence and subsequent decisions. The thresholds govern reviewed incidents, not the number of observers, and do not infer malicious intent automatically.

\subsection{Sustaining the trust community}
TrustSECO envisions companies funding the collection and verification of software trust data they need. A company allocates TrustCOIN to measurement requests, and contributors earn rewards by supplying observations that satisfy the community's verification rules. This connects demand for trustworthy software information with the effort required to produce and maintain it. Over time, the resulting evidence could also support other platforms and services.

TrustSECO implements funded rounds and reward distribution. Company purchasing and funding flows have not yet been demonstrated; the mechanism by which companies obtain TrustCOIN remains to be specified.

\section{Implementation and Demonstration}
\label{sec:implementation}
The platform connects three roles: contributors maintain observations about software, the community establishes agreement and handles disputes, and developers use the resulting assessments. Collectors and a coordination service connect external sources to a distributed ledger; the portal presents scores, community participation, and review history. Signed contributions link observations to their authors. The ledger records agreement, standing, and rewards so that an assessment can be explained through the decisions that produced it.

The demonstration follows a component through these roles. First, a developer examines its trust score and the considerations relevant to adoption. Second, the audience follows how contributor agreement supports the assessment and how disagreement leaves an input unresolved. Third, a reviewed contribution illustrates how the community can challenge an assessment and hold participants accountable. The portal provides the visible entry point for demonstrating these interactions; the intended package-manager workflow places them in the context of everyday dependency decisions.

TrustSECO's scope is the shared assessment process. It does not establish that all participants are independent, that upstream reports are complete, or that higher scores lead to better choices. These are distinct questions for security and user evaluation. Source code and setup instructions accompany the tool.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/SecureSECO/TrustSECO-Next}}

\section{Related Work}
The TrustSECO infrastructure builds on earlier work proposing a distributed software trust layer~\cite{hou2021trustseco}. The npmSECO evaluation~\cite{hou2024npmseco} provides prior evidence for presenting trust information during package selection. TrustSECO connects that interaction to a community that maintains and governs trust assessments.

\textbf{Distributed ledgers for software engineering.} Beller and Hejderup's ICSE-NIER vision~\cite{beller2019blockchain} connects distributed infrastructure, incentives, and trust in software engineering services. TrustSECO develops the connection between collective participation, incentives, and trust in dependency decisions.

\textbf{Measuring trust.} Boughton et al.~\cite{boughton2024decomposing} propose decomposing software supply-chain trust into relationships and behaviour-based indicators, questioning the grounding of convenient proxies such as stars. Their work motivates a distinction in TrustSECO: corroborating a measurement does not validate its interpretation as a trust indicator. TrustSECO complements this perspective with a community process for maintaining the inputs to assessments; the interpretation and weighting of indicators remain subjects for evaluation.

\textbf{Independent verification of packages.} Benedetti et al.~\cite{benedetti2025reproducible} study reproducible packaging across six open-source ecosystems. Their verification of correspondence between build inputs and delivered artifacts complements TrustSECO's repository measurements. Incorporating reproducible-build results would require a dedicated collection and verification policy.

\section{Research Agenda}
The next step is to bring this vision into everyday engineering and learn whether people want to make it their own.

\textbf{Trust in everyday package selection.} We want to revisit an npmSECO-style experiment~\cite{hou2024npmseco} in an open-source package manager, involving both developers and organizations. Do community-maintained assessments make them feel better informed and more confident about dependencies? Do prompts encourage exploration of alternatives? Interviews and configurable weight profiles should reveal what participants value, whether they want to adjust weights, and why. Comparing prompted and unprompted workflows should examine actual choices, replacement suitability, and decision effort alongside these experiences; confidence and switching alone do not establish better decisions.

\textbf{Trust lost and regained.} \emph{``Trust comes on foot, and leaves by horse.''} The scoring model makes this philosophy testable through severity-sensitive penalties, hard ceilings, and evidence-supported recovery. Evaluation must establish whether these settings prompt useful reassessment without unfairly disadvantaging well-maintained projects that disclose vulnerabilities. Known-exploited status is distinct from predicted exploitability; EPSS-based prioritization and deployment-specific reachability remain future work. Longitudinal studies should examine how disclosure, remediation, and community response influence the loss and recovery of developer trust.

\textbf{Sustainable funding and governance.} Can company-funded requests sustain useful participation without rewarding fabricated agreement or neglecting less-funded packages? Evaluation should cover reward allocation, data freshness, review effort, and appeals. Distributed governance must establish who can adjudicate disputed evidence and sanctions.

\textbf{Trust across the ecosystem.} How can community assessment extend across package ecosystems and incorporate reproducible builds, maintenance commitments, and other grounds for trust? Research should examine which assessments transfer between contexts and where communities need different criteria.

\section{Conclusion}
TrustSECO proposes community consensus as a foundation for software trust. It connects the people who assess software, the organizations that rely on it, and the decisions through which dependencies enter software systems. The platform makes this model concrete through shared assessment, accountable participation, and a score that can respond to trust gained and lost. Its longer-term ambition is for trust to become a maintained part of the software ecosystem: something developers can contribute to, organizations can interpret, and communities can strengthen together.
